\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLMs in game-theoretic settings.}
Recent work has systematically tested whether \LLM{}s can function as rational
strategic agents. Aher et al.\ \cite{Aher2023_using} used \LLM{}s to replicate
human subject behaviour in behavioural economics experiments. Brookins and
DeBacker \cite{Brookins2023_playing} and Guo \cite{Guo2023_gpt} investigated
whether \LLM{}s approximate Nash-rational play, finding mixed evidence across
game types. Akata et al.\ \cite{Akata2023_playing}
studied repeated normal-form games, while Fan et al.\
\cite{Fan2024_rational} demonstrated systematic failures when \LLM{}s are
prompted to output individual game actions. These limitations motivate our
strategy-generation approach, which operates at a higher level of abstraction
and separates policy specification from action execution.

\paragraph{LLMs in social dilemmas.}
Yocum et al.\ \cite{Yocum2023_mitigating}
and Piatti et al.\ \cite{Piatti2024_cooperate}
studied \LLM agent behaviour in Markov social dilemmas. Park et al.\
\cite{Park2023_generative} introduced generative agents for simulating social
behaviour. De Zarz{\`a} et al.\ \cite{DeZarza2023_emergent}
explored emergent cooperation in \LLM-extended coevolutionary theory.
Leibo et al.\ \cite{Leibo2017_multiagent} grounded such studies in
multi-agent reinforcement learning. Our contribution to this strand is the
analysis of \emph{evolutionary population dynamics}---how strategy types
rise or collapse across generations---rather than individual agent decisions
at a single time step.

\paragraph{The Willis et al.\ benchmark.}
Willis et al.\ \cite{Willis2025_llm_ipd} introduced the framework we extend.
They prompt \LLM{}s to generate natural-language \IPD strategies (converted to
Python), then simulate Moran evolutionary processes over populations of
attitude-agents (Aggressive, Cooperative, Neutral). Key findings include a
cooperative bias in both ChatGPT-4o and Claude~3.5~Sonnet, with Claude
exhibiting greater difficulty generating effective aggressive strategies and
notable fragility under action noise. The Refine prompt
\cite{Madaan2023_self_refine} narrowed this gap---demonstrating that
self-refinement can inadvertently enable aggressive strategies, a result that
complicates alignment-by-design assumptions.

\paragraph{Cross-generational and cross-provider comparisons.}
Prior capability comparisons across \LLM providers have focused on general
reasoning benchmarks such as MMLU or HumanEval, where cooperative dispositions
are absent by design. No published work applies this cross-provider lens to
evolutionary social dilemmas, where provider-level training choices and RLHF
objectives may differentially shape aggressive or cooperative tendencies. Equally
absent is any within-lineage longitudinal analysis: whether successive model
generations from the same provider preserve, amplify, or reverse the cooperation
patterns established by their predecessors. Our study targets both gaps.

\paragraph{Evolutionary game theory foundations.}
Our simulations rest on the Moran process \cite{Moran1958_random}, a classical
model of evolutionary selection in finite populations, and on the \IPD framework
\cite{Axelrod1984_evolution,Axelrod1981_evolution}. Nowak
\cite{Nowak2006_evolutionary} provides the backbone for convergence analysis in
finite evolutionary games. The noise mechanism follows Wu and Axelrod \cite{WuAxelrod1995_noise}
and Wahl and Nowak \cite{WahlNowak1999_noise}.
These threads---\LLM strategic behaviour, multi-agent social dilemmas, and
evolutionary population dynamics---jointly define the space this paper occupies:
the first cross-provider benchmark for next-generation \LLM{}s evaluated under
Darwinian selection over \IPD strategy populations.
